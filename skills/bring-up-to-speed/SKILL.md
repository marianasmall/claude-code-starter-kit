# Bring Up to Speed

Assemble everything the available record holds about a topic into a briefing for a specific reader — so they spend their time deciding, not reconstructing history.

## Two questions before any research

1. **Who is the reader, exactly?** What do they already know, what were they part of, and what decision are they facing? The briefing's job is the gap between what the record holds and what they hold.
2. **What should they see?** If the material includes private, sensitive, or third-party information the reader shouldn't have (someone else's finances, confidential terms, private commentary), leave it out deliberately and note the omission to the person requesting the brief — never resolve that silently.

## Gathering the record

Use whatever sources this conversation can reach: connected email, calendar, or document tools if available; otherwise ask the user to paste threads, attach files, or forward the key documents. Work newest-first (the recent three weeks establish current status), then backfill the timeline.

When asking the user which sources to check, ask for ALL the platforms that hold project state — not just email. Prompt with categories, because people under-name their tools: email and calendar, chat (Slack, Teams), project trackers (Monday, Asana, Jira), databases and wikis (Airtable, Notion), document drives, and meeting-recording tools. A briefing built from email alone misses every decision that happened in chat.

As you gather, establish the **visibility window**: when does the record's coverage begin, and when did the reader first get access? The gap between those two dates is the heart of the brief. Everything before the coverage date is a blind spot — name it, don't guess at it.

## Weigh sources honestly

Not all sources are equal. Strongest first:

1. **Original messages and documents** — primary; a person's email signature beats a contact list for details.
2. **Meeting notes and AI transcripts** — useful but cleaned-up; never treat as verbatim quotes, and check name spellings against email addresses (transcription mangles proper nouns).
3. **Published, citable sources** — for external facts (rules, organizations, public records); cite them.
4. **Spreadsheets and imported lists** — unverified until corroborated; say so.
5. **Anyone's memory** — weakest; verify before it enters the brief.

Any claim that would shape the reader's decision but rests only on a transcript or a recollection: verify it against a stronger source, or flag it plainly as unverified.

## The briefing shape

Lead with the bottom line, then support it:

- **BLUF** (bottom line up front): the state of play in three sentences — what's true, what's blocked, and on whom the next move waits.
- **Timeline** of who was waiting on whom at each step, with the blind-spot period visibly marked.
- **People map**: who's who, what each person holds, how to reach them.
- **Open items**: owner, item, and how long it's been sitting.
- **"What we'd still want to know"**: the honest list of what the record can't answer and who holds each missing piece.

Two optional companions when the reader has a next conversation to run:

- **A call agenda**: numbered asks, each with one line on why it matters, plus a fill-in notes section.
- **A ready-to-send email** to whoever owes the reader answers — written in the reader's own voice, questions numbered so they can be answered inline, urgency tied to a real date.

## Before delivering

Check the brief against itself: every figure identical everywhere it appears, ages computed from actual dates, and the BLUF consistent with the body. A brief that contradicts itself gets ignored.

And a boundary rule: working notes, drafts, and your research scraps never ship alongside the brief. The reader gets exactly what's for the reader.
